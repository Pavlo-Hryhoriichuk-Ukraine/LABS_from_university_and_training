For decades, theoretical physicists treated the interior of a black hole as a boundary where standard physical laws simply gave up. When Jacob Bekenstein and Stephen Hawking began exploring what happens when quantum particles fall across an event horizon, they found that the entropy grew in lock step with the surface area, not the volume, as you would expect. It was an unsettling conclusion for anyone trained in traditional thermodynamics, where doubling the volume of a box naturally doubles the number of microstates inside it.

Leonard Susskind, a physicist at Stanford University, built on their result in the 1990s and proposed that the black hole was literally a hologram, that everything happening inside can be observed from the outside. In some sense, the interior was superfluous. “I thought it was a little bit crazy,” Susskind said, “but I thought it was the least crazy of all the possibilities.” Gerard ’t Hooft, a Nobel laureate, and Charles Thorn, a physicist at the University of Florida in Gainesville, came to similar conclusions around the same time.

I’ve always found this black hole entropy argument compelling, because any patch of space can become a black hole if you put enough mass into it. Despite their reputation for weirdness, black holes are representative examples of space. They just have a way of bringing space’s stranger properties to the fore. So if a black hole is holographic, and any region of space can become a black hole, then, the argument goes, even the room you’re sitting in should be holographic. “It’s completely general,” Susskind said.

In the 1990s, physicist Leonard Susskind conceived of black holes as literal holograms, determining that you could know the inside merely by measuring the surface. This argument has a rock-solid universality, but I’ve also heard physicists describe holography as a speculative idea with an uncertain connection to reality. So I called up Latham Boyle, a physicist at the Higgs Centre for Theoretical Physics at the University of Edinburgh, hoping for an alternative view. He did not disappoint. 

Boyle doesn’t dispute Bekenstein and Hawking’s black hole findings, but he does question the holographic interpretation. He suspects that the act of putting a surface around a region of space, as happens when a black hole forms, creates two distinct types of entropy. One entropy tells you how many particles can fit inside, and that really does depend on the volume.

The existence of the surface gives you a second, “entanglement” entropy. Particles inside share a quantum connection, known as entanglement, with those outside; the bigger the surface, the more entanglement crosses it. The entanglement entropy depends on the area, not the volume. They’re not, Boyle posits, the same thing. 

“That seems like a less mystical, more down-to-earth interpretation of what’s going on,” he said.   But it helps holography that there is a second, more conceptually airtight finding behind it: AdS/CFT. AdS/CFT asks us to imagine a universe that is not like our own, one that curves in such a way that its infinite expanse of space can be pictured as fitting inside a finite snow globe. That might sound like a big ask, but it’s one that mathematicians, and mathematically minded artists such as M.C. Escher, are perfectly comfortable with. 

This geometry is known as anti-de Sitter (AdS) space.   When you construct a gravitational theory inside this hyperbolic volume, every particle collision occurring deep in the interior can be translated directly into the language of ordinary quantum fields dancing on the boundary. Juan Maldacena, who formulated the duality in 1997, essentially provided an exact translation manual between two seemingly incompatible worlds. 

“The geometry of the interior is an emergent feature,” Maldacena has pointed out in various lectures, arguing that gravity itself is not fundamental, but rather an effective description arising from the chaotic entanglement of quantum particles living on the outer shell.Yet the question that lingers across modern physics departments is whether this mathematical trick tells us anything about the actual universe we inhabit. Our cosmos is expanding, driven outward by a positive cosmological constant, which gives it the geometry of de Sitter space rather than the cozy, reflective boundaries of an anti-de Sitter snow globe. 

“If you try to put a boundary at infinity in our universe, the math immediately rebels,” explained one researcher working on quantum cosmology. Without the safe reflective walls of AdS space, gravity refuses to be boxed into a simple holographic code. Some physicists view this as a sign that holography is merely a toy model, a mathematical curiosity that works only in universes that look nothing like our own. Others, however, insist that the clues coming from black hole horizons are too sharp to ignore. If space itself is woven from quantum correlations, then whether that space is flat, hyperbolic, or expanding may simply be a matter of how the underlying information is entangled.


This tension between theoretical beauty and observational cosmology has pushed physicists to search for new mathematical machinery that does not rely on the artificial crutch of reflective boundaries. In recent years, attention has turned toward the quantum properties of the horizon itself, particularly the microscopic mechanisms that allow information to leak back out into the cosmos as a black hole slowly radiates away. When Don Page calculated the theoretical curve of entanglement entropy for an evaporating black hole in nineteen ninety-three, he showed that if information is preserved, the entropy of the radiation must initially rise, reach a maximum at the halfway point of the black hole's life, and then steadily drop back down to zero as the final particles escape.

For more than two decades, nobody knew how semiclassical gravity could possibly reproduce the downward slope of the Page curve. Standard calculations using Hawking's framework simply showed the entropy rising indefinitely, signaling an irreversible loss of information into the void. The breakthrough arrived only recently through the discovery of quantum extremal surfaces, often referred to as entanglement islands. Theorists realized that when calculating the fine-grained entropy of the outgoing Hawking radiation at late times, a subtle gravitational saddle point emerges deep inside the black hole interior.

This island region, located entirely behind the horizon, suddenly detaches from the rest of the interior geometry and becomes quantum-mechanically encoded into the distant radiation field. Once the island forms, the entropy calculation correctly follows the downward trajectory predicted by Page, confirming that the outgoing radiation retains the complete quantum blueprint of everything that ever fell inside. The interior degrees of freedom do not vanish; they simply get teleported across spacetime via the dense web of entanglement connecting the interior to the external Hawking particles.

While this calculation represents a monumental victory for the preservation of quantum information, it leaves researchers with an even deeper conceptual riddle about the nature of physical reality. If the interior of a black hole can be mathematically reconstructed from a cloud of distant photons drifting across empty space, the traditional notion of spatial locality loses all absolute meaning. Two points that appear separated by light-years in the classical metric can nonetheless act as adjacent neighbors through the non-local connections of quantum mechanics. As theoretical models continue to blur the distinction between geometric distance and informational correlation, physics is inexorably converging on the view that space itself is not an immutable container where events unfold, but a secondary, statistical illusion generated by the entanglement of deeper, non-spatial degrees of freedom.
